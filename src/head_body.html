Core biopsy of a breast lump

You've been told you need an ultrasound-guided breast core biopsy. Here's what that means.

A core biopsy takes a few small samples of tissue from a lump in your breast, so a pathologist can look at them under a microscope and work out what the lump is. This page explains what it is, what happens on the day, what you'll feel, and what comes after.

1 What a breast lump actually is

A breast lump is a clump of cells that has grown differently from the tissue around it. It can be a solid area, a sac of fluid (a cyst), or normal glandular tissue. The breast is made of glands, fat, and the skin that covers it.

Most breast lumps are not cancer — that means non-cancerous. But some are, and no one can tell just by feeling your breast or looking at a scan. That is why your doctor has asked for this test.

Breast lumps are common, and most are not cancer. Most people with one are simply monitored, but a small number need a sample taken so it can be checked — that is what the core biopsy does.

2 Why you need this test

The point of the biopsy is to take a small piece of the lump and find out exactly what it is, and to try to rule out cancer in your breast.

A core biopsy is the standard first test for this. It gives your doctor real information — enough to decide whether you need treatment, further tests, or simply to be watched.

4 What you'll feel

This is what people worry about most, so here it is honestly:

  • Pressure on your breast from the ultrasound probe resting there.
  • A quick pin prick, then a stinging that lasts a few seconds as the anaesthetic goes in.
  • A click each time the needle fires.
  • Some pressure as the samples are taken. You may feel very little. Most people describe it as quick, and less uncomfortable than they expected.

You'll be asked to stay as still as you can and to avoid coughing, talking or swallowing while the needle is in, because those movements shift the probe and make accurate placement harder.

5 How it's done

The biopsy takes a small core of tissue from the lump with a spring-loaded needle. Each pass removes one sample, and the device clicks as it fires. Usually three to six passes are made.

It is carried out under ultrasound guidance so the needle is placed accurately — using the same scanning that found the lump.

A local anaesthetic is injected to numb the area. It feels like a quick pin prick, then a stinging that lasts a few seconds, before the area goes numb.

6 Before the procedure

Usually no preparation is needed.

Tell your doctor if you are on any blood thinners or antiplatelets — medicines such as warfarin, rivaroxaban, aspirin or clopidogrel. Tell them before the procedure, and do not stop any of these on your own. Your doctor will say whether any should be paused, and when to restart them.

Tell your doctor about any blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines you take.
You do not normally need to stop eating or drinking beforehand.
Wear something comfortable that lets your breast be reached easily, and bring previous breast imaging.
Write down your questions — nothing is too small to ask.

7 Risks and complications

A core biopsy is generally safe. Minor effects such as slight pain, bruising or swelling are common but usually settle quickly; serious complications are rare. These are the risks your doctor wants you to know about:

Extremely rare. The needle is short and guided by scan, so it does not reach the lung.
Extremely rare
Up to about 20% of core biopsy results may come back as non-diagnostic or inadequate, because not enough cells were taken for the pathologist to make a clear diagnosis. A lump can also be missed or underestimated. If either happens, the usual next step is to repeat the biopsy — another small sample is taken — and your doctor will discuss whether a surgical biopsy is needed.
These complications are not intended to be exhaustive and may vary depending on underlying medical condition. They are NOT meant to be, and CANNOT be, exhaustive.
A non-diagnostic result does not mean something is wrong. It means there were not enough cells to be certain; the test is usually repeated, and your doctor will discuss what to do next.

8 After the procedure

First 48 hours
Firm pressure is held on the site to stop bleeding, then a pressure dressing is left in place and kept dry for 48 hours. Do not remove it or get it wet.
Straight away
You can go back to usual, non-strenuous activities, including eating and drinking.
After about 2–3 hours
The anaesthetic wears off. Soreness for a few days is normal, and bruising can take 1–2 weeks to fade and may feel lumpy — that is normal.
As needed
Simple painkillers such as paracetamol are fine. Avoid ibuprofen, aspirin-type medicines and mefenamic acid, which increase bleeding and bruising.
For about 48 hours
Wear a supportive non-wired bra. Avoid gym, swimming and heavy lifting.
Seek immediate medical attention if
You have a sudden increase in pain or swelling, continued bleeding, a rise in temperature, or signs of infection at the site.

9 Getting your results

The results usually take about 2 weeks to process. You will be told them at a follow-up appointment.

It is important to attend the follow-up appointment so the results can be discussed properly with you. Waiting can feel long — that is normal, and the wait itself does not mean anything has gone wrong.

10 What if you'd rather not have it?

If you are not keen for a core biopsy, your doctor will not be able to find out exactly what the lump is or to rule out cancer within your breast. You should discuss alternative options for managing your breast lump with your doctor, e.g. watching the lump with regular ultrasound, or a surgical biopsy of the lump for testing. These options would be best discussed with your primary (referring) doctor.
There is a chance that the lump will turn out to be cancerous and you risk missing the necessary treatment.

If anything here is unclear or worrying, ask your doctor. Bring this page with you — it is easier to talk it through together.
